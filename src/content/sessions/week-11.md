This week works on `R-03`, `R-05`, `R-07`.

## In the studio

Before the field slot, the team writes down the one question this flight will answer and the predicted number it expects. Not a guess — the predicted value, from your own ledger, or from a calculation the team can explain. Write it on the bench so you know what the log is compared against when you read it afterwards. This week the question is usually one of stall speed, cruise power or endurance — whichever of `R-03`, `R-05` and `R-07` this flight is scheduled to close, and against your own airframe's own number, not the course design point. The number goes on a card taped to the transmitter, not in someone's head, because a prediction remembered after the flight has usually already been adjusted to fit what happened.

The aircraft goes on the bench for a final centre-of-gravity check in its actual flight-ready configuration. This is the weight and balance with the battery fitted, the servo pack attached, the antenna run, everything that flies. The bench fixture is two dowels set level at the datum; the aircraft balances across them by its wingtips, with the fuselage hanging free between. Everything added since the last CG check — a heavier battery, a repositioned receiver, a mount bolted on for this flight — moves the balance point, and the only way to catch that is to check it dressed exactly as it will fly, not stripped down for convenience. A CG check signed off weeks ago without the real flight setup can still be wrong, and it shows up on landing — a nose-in prop strike because the aircraft lands heavier than the bench check assumed. Check the CG again, where it lives.

The data logger comes off the aircraft in the workshop, not at the flight line, because scrolling through a flight log on a phone screen with the wind still blowing is how a transient gets missed. Plug the logger into the bench computer, pull the full trace, and look at it before anyone starts talking about how the flight felt. A flight that felt smooth from the ground can still show a current spike at motor start that nearly touched the pack's own limit, and a flight that felt rough can still have landed its one number exactly on the prediction. The felt account of a flight and its logged account frequently disagree, and the log wins the argument every time.

After the flight, read the log in the room. For each of `R-03`, `R-05` and `R-07`, you measured something and predicted something else. Every gap gets a written reason. Reading the current trace for the moment stall onset shows up is different from eyeballing a top number and moving on — you are comparing your prediction against the flight, not the flight against some vague expectation. Did the stall come early because the wing is smaller than your drawings show? Did cruise power sit lower because the propeller is more efficient in the real air than the thrust model assumed? The endurance number needs the same treatment across the whole flight, not just its final total — a pack that reads healthy at the twenty-minute mark and falls away sharply after that is telling you something different from a pack that declines smoothly the entire way, even if both land on the same final figure. Two teams can log the same measured stall speed and still be in different positions: one predicted it closely and one predicted it badly, and the team that missed has the harder job this session — saying why, in writing, before the sheet goes in the folder. Write the gap and the reason beside it.

## At the field

Your team books its own slot for this week — see [Field days](/field-days/) for
the rules and the windows. Every team's slot carries the same objective.

- **Field objective:** Fly a timed straight leg at cruise on your own airframe and log pack current and voltage against your predicted cruise power.
- **Owner:** Propulsion & Power
- **Predict:** Predict cruise power and stall speed from your own ledger, not from the course design point of 60 W.
- **Weather fallback:** A crosswind ruins a timed leg. Fly the leg into wind and back out, average the two, and say in the log that you did.
- **Safety:** The first flight of any airframe is flown by the instructor. Your team's test pilot takes it on the second flight, never the first.
