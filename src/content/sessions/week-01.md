## In the studio

Your team is four people, fixed from week 2, with a role assigned to each: chief engineer, aero and structures, avionics and electrical, test lead. This week you nominate the roles — the assignment locks properly next week, once everyone has spent a session in the workshop and has an opinion about which two subsystems suit them. A role owns two of the five subsystems — Aero, Structures, Propulsion & Power, Avionics & Control, Operations. The chief engineer owns none; the chief engineer coordinates the handovers between them, which makes the chief engineer the one person on the team whose job is entirely about what other people hand each other, not about a subsystem of their own.

The studio has 4 workshop-and-lab hours this week, and none of them go to building anything. You spend them getting your repository issued — a blank one, with nothing in it yet but the folders the course expects — meeting the lab technician who weighs every material sample your team brings in for the rest of the semester, and learning where the workshop's equipment lives: the scale, the bench vices, the covering iron, the box of servo leads nobody has claimed yet. You leave the session with a repository, a nominated but not locked role each, and a working idea of who else is on your team, because for some of you this is the first time you have spoken to them. You will visit the field to fly the objective below. Read the Field days page to book your own slot and understand the go/no-go and weather fallback rules.

Write down what you predict before the flight: how many seconds you think you will hold level flight on the buddy box. That number goes in the logbook before you fly, and the debrief compares the prediction to what happened — the number written down beforehand is the only one that counts as a prediction; anything written after the flight is a memory, which is a different thing. This is the only one of the four studios with no quiz attached — quizzes run weeks 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10 and 11 — but the habit of predicting before checking starts here anyway, informally, a week ahead of the first graded one.

None of this week's admin feels like engineering while you are doing it. Signing into a fresh repository, learning which drawer in the workshop holds the servo leads, writing a guess about seconds in a logbook before anyone has touched a transmitter — none of it produces a wing, a frame or a line of firmware. It still counts. A team that cannot get a repository issued and a role nominated in one sitting is not going to get a mass ledger opened and sourced by week 2's deadline either, and the studio is deliberately testing the smaller, boring version of that same competence first.

## At the field

Your team books its own slot for this week — see [Field days](/field-days/) for
the rules and the windows. Every team's slot carries the same objective.

- **Field objective:** Hold level flight on the buddy box for thirty seconds without the instructor taking the aircraft back.
- **Owner:** Operations
- **Predict:** Before you touch the transmitter, write down how many seconds you think you will hold it. Bring both numbers to the debrief.
- **Weather fallback:** Over fifteen knots across the strip the lesson moves to the simulator bay, and the buddy box runs against the sim instead.
- **Safety:** You do not hold a transmitter until the chief flying instructor has written your name on the day's flight line.

Everything on this list is Operations' problem, not because Operations is the most important subsystem but because it is the only one with anything built yet. The other four subsystems inherit the habits you form this week whether they like it or not: the same predict-before-you-check discipline shows up in the ledger from week 2 onward, and the same four-person team keeps the same five subsystems for the rest of the course.
