This week works on `R-01`, `R-07`, `R-08`.

## In the studio

In your workshop hours, you turn the number from the field into one sentence. You bring the three stopwatch splits and the circuit length back from the fence line, average them, and convert the time into a distance using the trainer's cruise speed — the same arithmetic you did at the field, checked again on the workshop bench where nobody is holding a stopwatch and a clipboard at the same time. For this team, the averaged number comes out at 240 m, and it goes into the mission sentence in the shape the course requires — a threshold with a unit attached, not an adjective: "the aircraft shall complete a mission leg of 240 m while carrying a 250 g payload." That sentence goes into your own team's requirements document, in your own team's repository, with an owner named among your four roles, before the field trip is over. It does not go into this course's requirement set — that file belongs to the course, not to any one team, and your mission sentence is not one of its fourteen lines. You also copy `R-01` into your own ledger as the mass cap it names, so the 1800 g figure sits next to your mission sentence instead of somewhere back in a lecture slide. No adjective stands in for a number you have not measured, and your team does not commit to a distance until the stopwatch says so: the whole point of doing the arithmetic again in workshop hours, on the same day as the field slot, is that nobody gets a week to talk the number down to something more comfortable to build.

In lab hours, you open the ledger file — a spreadsheet with one row per part and a column for the method, not just the number. Every line is an estimate with a method: the wing structure mass is estimated from a published glider design of similar span, scaled to your own wing area, and the scaling method goes in the method column — not a source document, a method, because a method is something the next person on your team can redo and a citation to a report nobody can find is something they can't. Every estimate sits on paper before it sits on a scale. Ask the lab technician to weigh a shelf part — a servo, a battery connector, a sheet of carbon — and enter that weight as a sourced reference figure, so at least one row in your ledger is already a measurement on day one rather than an estimate waiting to be checked. The 250 g payload is already in the ledger as a constraint from `R-08`: that is the only payload mass your configuration allows, and it is not yours to renegotiate no matter how the wing-sizing numbers come out later. You do not pick the mass budget's line items; you estimate them, source what you can this week, and live with what they add to for the rest of the course.

This is also the first week the two rooms mean different things. The workshop is where the team argues about wording — whether "mission leg" means one leg of the circuit or the whole out-and-back, whether the payload sits mid-fuselage or in the nose — until the argument produces a sentence everyone can point to. The lab is where the lab technician's scale settles arguments instead: a part either weighs what the estimate said or it doesn't, and if it doesn't, the ledger's method column is what has to change, not the wish that it weighed less.

By the end of the session your ledger holds three things that did not exist last week: a mission sentence with a real distance in it, `R-01` written in as the mass cap, and at least one row that is sourced rather than guessed. Nothing else in the ledger changes yet. The wing is still whatever shape somebody guessed at in week 1, and it stays that way until the review at the end of week 3 makes you defend it in front of the rest of the team.

## At the field

Your team books its own slot for this week — see [Field days](/field-days/) for
the rules and the windows. Every team's slot carries the same objective.

- **Field objective:** Time three circuits of a club trainer and turn the average into the length of mission leg your team will commit to.
- **Owner:** Operations
- **Predict:** Predict the circuit time from the trainer's cruise speed and the circuit dimensions before you start the stopwatch.
- **Weather fallback:** Rain: time the circuits off the club's logged videos instead, and write in the log that you are trusting somebody else's camera.
- **Safety:** Stand behind the flight line. Holding a stopwatch is not a reason to walk forward for a better view.
