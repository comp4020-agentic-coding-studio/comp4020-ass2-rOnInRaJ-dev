**By the end:** you can take a documented drone failure back to its cause and name the requirement that would have caught it.

A team flew a successful week 11 and returned both packs fully charged, ready for the fly-off. One pack sat in a team member's car during the mid-semester break — fourteen days fully charged, in a car boot, in summer heat. The team believed a pack sitting in a car is just a pack in a car. When they retrieved it for week 12, the pack had puffed visibly, swollen from internal gas pressure. A lithium polymer cell overcharged or stored at high temperature swells because the chemistry breaks down and produces gas. That pack was no longer safe to fly. The failure was not that the team lost a pack; the failure was that nobody wrote down the rule before the break started.

This is the shape every team's own case study takes in the final project: outcome, prior belief, cause, and the requirement question, in that order. Starting from the requirement and working forward produces a case study that reads like it was written to justify a rule that already existed. Starting from the outcome and working backward is harder, and is the actual exercise — you do not get to know which requirement will turn out to matter until you have worked out what happened and why the team believed what it believed beforehand.

Which requirement would have caught it? None of the fourteen, cleanly. `R-14` closes the loop on packs returned at the end of the course, discharged, to the club bin. It says nothing about storage in the middle. This is the point of the exercise — not every failure has a requirement waiting for it. Writing one after the fact is easy. Writing it before the fact, without sounding paranoid, and then enforcing it when nothing has gone wrong yet, is the actual skill a design team needs. Most of the fourteen requirements exist because an earlier failure, somewhere, made someone write one down; `R-14` itself is close to being that kind of after-the-fact fix, since it exists because packs used to not come back at all. Writing the storage rule this case study points toward, before this pack ever swelled, would have meant imagining a specific failure that had not happened yet. That is a different skill from closing a loop after the fact, and the harder one.

The fly-off runs a full day at Mount Sludge. Every team flies the mission it wrote in week 3, with the payload fitted, closing `R-08`. Every team also flies its signature capability — the thing this aircraft does that the others do not. Your team chose that capability in week 4 and designed the aircraft around it. It is not assigned; it is yours. Past examples have included a mass-fraction contest entry, slowest stable flight, maximum glide range from a fixed release height, and a precision drop. The scoring runs one aircraft at a time, measured against the prediction you logged before the propeller turned. The flight line runs one aircraft at a time all day, with every other team's aircraft grounded and packs disconnected while a scoring flight is in the air. A team gets one scored attempt at the mission and one at its signature capability; a card taped to the transmitter carries both predictions, written down before the first flight of the day, checked against the log afterwards the same way every flight this semester has been checked.

`R-14` has no engineering content. It is bookkeeping. Every pack comes back discharged to the club bin, signed off in the logbook, closing the loop on every pack the course issued. That is absolute. It is not a design requirement; it is a disposal rule, and it stays. The bin itself is a lockable cabinet in the clubhouse, not a bench somebody might forget to lock. A discharged pack that never makes it there is, as far as the club's own records are concerned, still an issued pack, sitting somewhere, ageing the same way the one in this story did.

## Outline

- one real, documented failure, worked back from the outcome to the cause
- which requirement would have caught it, and why nobody wrote that requirement
- the fly-off: mission, signature capability, scoring
- disposal: what `R-14` is for, and why it has no engineering content at all
